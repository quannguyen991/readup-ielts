exploded. This phenomenon, often called the "Analog Resurgence", is not simply about nostalgia for a

In the digital realm, everything is perfect, instant, and ephemeral. A smartphone photo is sharp, 
colorful, and exists mostly as data on a server. If you don't like it, you take ten more. In contrast, analog 
media imposes scarcity. A roll of film has only 24 or 36 exposures. Each shot costs money and cannot be

The trend extends beyond photography. Music streaming services offer convenience, granting

Perhaps the most extreme version of this movement is the return of the "dumb phone". Some 
teenagers are trading their smartphones for basic flip phones that can only make calls and send texts. They 
are voluntarily cutting themselves off from the algorithm. By removing the constant ping of notifications 
and the infinite scroll of TikTok, they report feeling less anxious and more connected to the people right in 
front of them. They are not rejecting technology entirely; they are simply choosing to be its master rather 
than its servant. 
Ultimately, the analog resurgence is about control and tangibility. In a world where we own fewer 
physical things and subscribe to more digital services, holding a physical photograph or a vinyl record 
provides a necessary anchor. It reminds us that some experiences are worth waiting for, and that sometimes, 
the best way to move forward is to look backward.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE AND THE FUTURE OF CREATIVITY 
A 
The release of advanced generative AI tools has sent shockwaves through the creative industries. For the 
first time in history, computer algorithms can generate intricate paintings, write compelling essays, and 
compose music that rivals human output in a matter of seconds. This technological leap has forced society 
to re-evaluate the very definition of creativity. Previously thought to be the exclusive domain of the human 
mind, artistic expression is now being mimicked by machines trained on vast datasets of existing works. 
This shift has polarized opinions: some see it as the dawn of a new artistic renaissance, while others view it 
as the beginning of the end for human professions. 
B 
Proponents of this technology emphasize the concept of "democratization". By lowering the barrier to entry, 
AI allows individuals without formal training to visualize concepts and share ideas that were previously 
locked in their imagination. For small business owners, independent game developers, and hobbyists, these 
tools provide a cost-effective way to produce high-quality assets. Furthermore, for professional artists, AI 
can serve as a powerful brainstorming partner. Instead of starting with a blank canvas, a designer can 
generate dozens of rough concepts in minutes, selecting the best ones to refine manually. In this view, AI 
is not a replacement, but a catalyst for efficiency.

However, the rapid adoption of AI has raised serious ethical and economic concerns. The primary anxiety 
among illustrators, writers, and musicians is the threat of redundancy. If a corporation can generate an 
advertising campaign for a fraction of the cost using software, the demand for entry-level creative jobs may 
plummet. Moreover, there is the contentious issue of copyright. AI models learn by analyzing billions of 
images and texts scraped from the internet, often without the original creators' consent. Many artists argue 
that their distinct styles are being replicated and commercialized by tech companies without compensation, 
leading to calls for stricter legal frameworks. 
D 
Beyond the economic arguments lies a deeper philosophical debate about the nature of art itself. Critics 
argue that while AI can mimic the aesthetics of art, it lacks the "soul" or emotional intent that defines human 
expression. A poem written by a machine is a statistical prediction of words, devoid of lived experience, 
pain, or joy. Therefore, while AI art might be visually stunning, it may fail to resonate on a human level in 
the way a masterpiece born from personal struggle does. This perspective suggests that human connection 
is an intrinsic value of art that algorithms cannot replicate, no matter how sophisticated they become. 
E 
In response to these challenges, a new hybrid approach is emerging. Rather than rejecting the technology 
entirely, many creative professionals are finding ways to integrate AI into their workflows while 
maintaining their unique voice. We are seeing the rise of "AI-assisted" art, where the human remains the 
director, guiding the algorithm to achieve a specific vision. Educational institutions are also beginning to 
adapt, shifting their focus from teaching technical skills which machines can now handle to teaching 
conceptual thinking, curation, and emotional intelligence. The focus is moving from "how to create" to 
"what to create and why". 
F 
Looking forward, the integration of AI in creativity seems inevitable, but its trajectory will depend on 
regulation and societal adaptation. Governments are currently scrambling to establish guidelines regarding 
transparency, requiring AI-generated content to be labeled and intellectual property rights. Ultimately, the 
future landscape of creativity will likely be defined not by a battle between humans and machines, but by 
how well humans can harness these new tools to tell their stories. The most successful artists of the future 
may well be those who can bridge the gap between algorithmic precision and human empathy.

semiconductor ecosystem by 2030, five experts weigh in on the feasibility, risks, and strategic trade-offs of

--- PAGE 10 ---
A 
The government’s target of training 50,000 semiconductor engineers by 2030 is a bold declaration of intent, 
but we must be wary of the "quantity trap". Producing thousands of fresh graduates is futile if we cannot 
offer them competitive livelihoods. Currently, a mid-level engineer in our country earns significantly less 
than their counterpart in Singapore or Taiwan. This wage arbitrage, while attractive to foreign investors, 
acts as a centrifuge for local talent. We are witnessing a "brain drain" not just across borders, but internally 
where the best minds are siphoned off by FDI firms to do peripheral tasks, leaving domestic R&D labs 
empty. Unless we solve the salary structure and career progression dilemma, we are essentially subsidizing 
the workforce for other nations’ economies. 
B 
While the fervor focuses on chips, the elephant in the room is infrastructure resilience. Semiconductor 
fabrication plants (Fabs) are notoriously "thirsty" and "power-hungry". The power outages in the north 
during the summer of 2023 were a wake-up call; a fab cannot tolerate even a millisecond of power 
fluctuation without risking millions in ruined wafers. The revised Power Development Plan (PDP8) hints 
at a return to nuclear power and massive grid upgrades, which is a tacit admission that renewable energy 
alone cannot provide the baseload stability required. Furthermore, the immense water usage of fabrication 
plants places them on a collision course with agricultural needs in climate-vulnerable deltas. If we do not 
secure our utilities, the "silicon dream" could dry up quite literally. 
C 
We must recognize that the "China + 1" strategy adopted by multinational corporations is the primary wind 
in our sails. This is a geopolitical gift, not necessarily an endorsement of our current readiness. Global tech 
giants are urgently seeking a "neutral" sanctuary to de-risk their supply chains from trade tensions. This 
provides our country with a golden, yet narrowing, window of time. However, this influx is not guaranteed 
to last forever. As competitors like India and Malaysia aggressively roll out subsidies, our advantage of 
political stability and neutrality must be complemented by rapid execution. We are in a race against time to 
embed ourselves so deeply in the global value chain that we become indispensable before the geopolitical 
winds shift again. 
D 
The greatest danger lies in the "Middle-Income Trap". Currently, our successes are largely in the ATP 
(Assembly, Testing, and Packaging) sector. While this creates jobs, it captures the least value in the 
semiconductor smile curve. If we remain satisfied with being the world's packaging factory, we are merely 
upgrading from sewing shirts to soldering circuit boards - the structural economics remain those of low- 
wage labor. True economic sovereignty comes from "Design" and owning the Intellectual Property (IP). 
The strategic formula must force a migration upstream. We need to nurture fabless startups that design 
chips, rather than just hosting foreign plants that assemble them. Otherwise, we will grow old before we 
grow rich. 
E 
Not all regions should follow the same playbook. The Central Region’s emerging strategy offers a 
fascinating case study in "asymmetric competition". Realizing it cannot compete with the North’s industrial 
scale or the South’s established ecosystem, the central region is leveraging specific legislative tools like the 
recent National Assembly resolutions to create a policy "sandbox". By offering radical tax holidays and 
establishing Free Trade Zones focused on logistics and testing equipment imports, they are bypassing the 
heavy manufacturing route to target the "design" niche. This approach minimizes the burden on land and 
energy while maximizing the value of human capital. It is a calculated bet that attracting a cluster of 
boutique design firms is more sustainable for a tourism-centric city than building massive factories.
