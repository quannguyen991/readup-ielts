Section 1: For questions 41-50, read the following passage and choose the best answer (A, B, C or 
D) to indicate the correct answer to each of the questions. (1,0 point) 
The concept of a “growth mindset”, introduced by psychologist Carol Dweck, has transformed the 
way educators think about student achievement. At its core, the theory distinguishes between two 
fundamental belief systems: a fixed mindset, in which individuals believe their abilities are innate 
and unchangeable, and a growth mindset, in which people believe that intelligence and talent can be 
developed through hard work, dedication, and learning from failure. 
Research consistently shows that students who adopt a growth mindset tend to perform better 
academically over time. When faced with a challenging task, they are more likely to persist rather 
than give up, viewing setbacks as valuable learning opportunities rather than evidence of inadequacy. 
In contrast, students with a fixed mindset often avoid difficult challenges to protect their self-image, 
fearing that failure will expose their supposed limitations. 
Teachers play a pivotal role in nurturing a growth mindset. The way feedback is delivered can 
significantly influence a student's beliefs about their own potential. Praising effort and strategy, rather 
than natural ability, encourages students to embrace challenges and remain motivated even when 
progress is slow. Phrases such as “You worked really hard on this” are considerably more effective 
than “You are so clever.”

Importantly, a growth mindset does not mean that everyone can achieve anything with enough 
effort. Rather, it suggests that one's starting point is not a ceiling. When students understand that the 
brain can form new neural connections through practice, they become more resilient learners. Schools 
that embed this philosophy into their culture report higher levels of student engagement and a notable 
reduction in academic anxiety.

Volunteering is widely regarded as one of the most enriching activities a young person can engage 
in. By giving their time and energy to help others, teenagers not only contribute positively to their 
communities but also gain skills and perspectives that cannot be taught in a classroom. Research 
carried out by the University of Edinburgh in 2021 found that students who volunteered regularly 
reported significantly higher levels of empathy and self-confidence compared to those who did not. 
Despite its many benefits, volunteering rates among teenagers have declined in several countries 
over the past decade. Academics and youth workers cite increasing academic pressure and the 
growing appeal of digital entertainment as the primary reasons for this trend. Some organisations 
have responded by introducing flexible, short-term volunteering opportunities that can fit around a 
student's school schedule. 
There is also debate about whether schools should make volunteering compulsory. Supporters 
argue that mandatory community service builds character and a sense of civic responsibility. Critics, 
however, contend that forcing young people to volunteer may undermine the spirit of the activity, 
turning a selfless act into an obligation that students resent rather than embrace.

about 50-60 words, using your own words. (1,0 point) 
Peer pressure refers to the influence that people of the same age group exert on one another, either 
encouraging or discouraging certain behaviours. For teenagers, this social force is particularly 
powerful, as the desire to fit in and gain acceptance from peers is one of the strongest motivators 
during adolescence. Peer pressure can take both negative and positive forms. On the negative side, it 
may lead young people to engage in risky behaviours such as smoking, substance abuse, or skipping

--- PAGE 6 ---
school, simply to avoid rejection or ridicule. On the positive side, however, peer pressure can inspire 
teenagers to study harder, adopt healthy habits, or pursue ambitious goals when surrounded by 
motivated and high-achieving friends. The key to handling peer pressure effectively lies in developing 
a strong sense of personal identity and the confidence to make independent decisions. Young people 
who have supportive families and a clear understanding of their own values are considerably better 
equipped to resist harmful influences while benefiting from the positive aspects of peer 
encouragement.
